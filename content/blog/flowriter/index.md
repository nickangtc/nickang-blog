Flowriter was my attempt to build a modern writer's laptop: a dedicated machine for drafting words without the distractions of a general-purpose computer.

The idea came from my own frustration with writing on laptops. A MacBook could do everything, which meant it constantly tempted me away from the thing I had sat down to do. A [typewriter](/2016-03-05-impression-of-a-millennial-typewriter/) had the opposite problem: romantic, focused, but too cumbersome for modern work.

I wanted something in between.

So I started a hardware startup around it. I sketched, prototyped, talked to people in the maker scene, explored grants, and tried to find a way to turn the idea into a real product.

Eventually, [I shelved it](/2016-03-12-hardware-startup-why-shelf/).

The market felt too small, hardware was too complex, and the bigger realisation was that I was trying to build a technical product without enough technical depth. That discomfort pushed me toward [learning programming properly](/2017-03-12-general-assembly-singapore-review/), which eventually became my [career in software engineering](/2021-07-04-reflecting-on-my-career-in-tech-5-years-in/).

In that sense, Flowriter failed as a startup but succeeded as a turning point.

It taught me that I did not just want to have ideas about tools. I wanted to be able to build them.
