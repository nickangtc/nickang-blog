Chef is one of my favourite movies. I always get new thoughts watching it - the hallmark of a great show.

This time, I rewatched it (third time?) for a very specific reason: to feel what it's like to be in the chef Casper's shoes, being really good at what he does, but having his agency smothered every day by a restaurant owner (his boss) who is single-minded about profit.

But why? How does this relate to my life?

It's hard to say. I certainly don't think I'm as good at what I do (software engineering) as Jon Favreau's chef is good at culinary arts. For now, I'm neither as good nor as passionate about making software as he is at cooking food.

So let's just say I want to feel something to spark new thoughts. This post is meant to elucidate those thoughts.

1. Cooking food has a delightful property that can also be found in making software: a tight feedback loop. When you finish cooking a dish, you serve it to a diner, and you can see their reaction. From that, you learn what adjustments you should make. Software is similar.
2. Cooking's feedback loop is, however, tighter than software's. In a single seating, you get the satisfaction of creating something and learning from the end-to-end process of preparing, cooking, serving, and observing/asking. Only a certain kind of in-person software will have this tight feedback loop, since software typically isn't served by hand in a single location (like a restaurant) but more like thousands.
3. "I get to touch people's lives with what I do." An emerging theme in my current stage of life is a stronger desire to do work that can be experienced in-person, where this feedback loop is as tight as possible, like a food truck cook and his hungry patrons. I want to see what my work is doing for people.
4. Speaking of hunger, I wonder what kind of person is hungry for software. And what kind of software? Also, where are these people? It sure would be nice to work with people hungry to know about/use the software I'm making. It would be mean not having to sell as much and focusing on the quality/performance of the product.
5. I can now "cook" new software features within a day. The last 10 months of building [Youtionary](/youtionary/) and [Album](/album/) using agentic engineering tools has opened those doors for many engineers. So in terms of iteration speed, I'm much closer to a cook now than before.
6. Neither the chef nor the restaurant owner are wrong or right. They're just optimising for different metrics (owner is optimising for profit; chef is optimising for gustatory satisfaction).
7. In the early parts of the movie, the chef himself was actually convinced by the owner to serve the menu for the masses to the food critic, because the owner's commercial argument actually made sense. People loved his menu! Why take the risk of switching it up, even if there was a food critic coming tonight?
8. At this point ~10 years into my software engineering career, I've been so far away from the user (my equivalent of a chef's diner) that I don't even how it feels to "touch people's lives with what I do." But I'm very curious how much that would move me into doing my best work.
9. Friends at work is possible and highly desirable, but seems to me to be only attainable in a small company (size of a kitchen). Another prerequisite is that each person on the team needs to carry their own part of the process of making the food (chef de cuisine, sous chef, line cook, server, head waitress, etc.)
